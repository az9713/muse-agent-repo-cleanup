01The setup

Ten tabs, one pipeline

On September 17, 2026, ten YouTube links arrived across the day in a chat window. Each one traveled the same path: identify the video, get the transcript, write the summary in the page’s section template, add a tab button and panel to index.html, bump the header (“Eight videos, distilled.” became “Nine”, then “Ten”) and the tabs CSS grid (repeat(8, …) → repeat(9, …) → repeat(10, …)), push to main on az9713/today_09172026, and verify the live page at az9713.github.io/today_09172026/ renders every tab.

The pipeline’s second stage is the one that can fail, and the one this report is about. Everything downstream — the prose, the tab, the push — is mechanical. The transcript is the hard part: either YouTube hands over the words, or you have to go get them somewhere else, or you have to say honestly that you couldn’t.

The invariant: no section ships without a source-note stating what the summary was grounded in. That rule is load-bearing — it is what makes the other five stages trustworthy.
02The capture

Ask YouTube for the words first

Route one is the obvious one: ask YouTube for the captions. yt-dlp can pull both creator-uploaded subtitles and the auto-generated captions YouTube produces for most videos. When it works, it works completely — one 62-minute episode yielded 1,734 caption lines, the entire conversation, ready to read.

The sandbox had one quirk of its own before YouTube even got a say. The first attempts died on an SSL failure: traffic was passing through a sandbox proxy whose certificate the client did not trust. The fix was a flag, --no-check-certificate — a compromise acceptable here because the threat model is a developer sandbox, not a payment terminal, but worth naming plainly rather than hiding in a command line.

$ yt-dlp --no-check-certificate \
  --write-subs --write-auto-subs --sub-langs en \
  "https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=38vwjWpHFes"

✓ doomed.en.srt — 120KB, 1,734 lines, full 62 minutes

When this route succeeds, the rest of the pipeline is honest and straightforward: a summary written from the actual transcript, with a source-note that names the transcript as the source. The trouble is that it only sometimes succeeds.

03The block

The bot-wall is not personal

YouTube’s anti-bot detection watches for data-center IP addresses, and the sandbox is one. The first symptom is HTTP Error 429: Too Many Requests. If requests keep coming, the wall escalates to a hard stop:

$ yt-dlp --write-auto-subs --sub-langs en "https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lrsW8k-dHSk"
WARNING: [youtube] lrsW8k-dHSk: Unable to download webpage: HTTP Error 429: Too Many Requests
ERROR: [youtube] lrsW8k-dHSk: Sign in to confirm you’re not a bot. Use --cookies-from-browser or --cookies for the authentication.

The blocking is inconsistent by design. One video’s captions download cleanly; the next is refused; a third fails only after several attempts. The practical consequence is that aggressive retry loops make the block worse — every failed attempt deepens the hole, so hammering a dead endpoint is the one strategy guaranteed not to work.

When the front door closed, the researchers tried the side doors, one by one: the Invidious captions API (HTTP 200, empty body), the Piped API (502 or dead), the video.google.com/timedtext endpoint (empty), public aggregator sites (client-side or empty), the Invidious audio proxy (Cloudflare’s own bot-wall), and a direct googlevideo audio URL (IP-blocked; one download stalled at ~1.8 MB and was killed). Every route led back to the same gate. That exhaustive failure was useful information: it meant the fallback had to live outside YouTube entirely.

The lesson of the block: budget your attempts. Two careful tries tell you as much as twenty — and the twentieth is the one that gets the IP blacklisted for the rest of the day.
04The fallback

If the show has a podcast, YouTube doesn’t matter

The fallback that actually worked bypasses YouTube completely. Many video shows are also podcasts: their RSS feeds carry the identical episode as a plain MP3 over HTTP, no bot-wall, no sign-in, no JavaScript runtime. The procedure is simple — fetch the show’s RSS, find the matching episode, download the audio, and transcribe it locally with faster-whisper.

Two of the day’s ten tabs were built this way. The Dina Powell McCormick All-In episode, whose YouTube captions were blocked, was transcribed from the identical episode audio in the official All-In Podcast feed. The a16z real-time-AI-video conversation — released on YouTube as “How Real-Time AI Video Is Changing How Creators Work” — was transcribed from The a16z Show feed, where the same conversation appeared as “The Next Frontier of AI Video Is Control”. Both sections say so in their source-notes, naming the podcast feed as the transcript’s origin.

The fallback had one genuine environment gotcha. The transcription model download crashed inside httpx 0.28.1 while parsing the sandbox’s no_proxy variable, because it contained the IPv6 literal [::1]. The fix: unset no_proxy and NO_PROXY before the download step. It is the kind of bug that looks like a network failure and is actually a string-parsing failure — worth one line in the runbook.

$ unset no_proxy NO_PROXY
$ curl -s "$SHOW_RSS" | grep -i episode-title # match the episode
$ curl -o episode.mp3 "$MP3_URL" # plain HTTP, no bot-wall
$ faster-whisper episode.mp3 --language en # transcribe locally

One more safeguard became part of the procedure mid-day, after a researcher nearly summarized the wrong episode — reading a SemiAnalysis transcript for a URL that belonged to a different creator. The cheapest identity check in the pipeline is YouTube’s oEmbed endpoint, which returns a video’s title and author with no authentication at all:

$ curl -s "https://www.youtube.com/oembed?url=https://www.youtube.com/watch?v=lrsW8k-dHSk&format=json" \
  | python3 -c "import json,sys; d=json.load(sys.stdin); print(d['title'],'|',d['author_name'])"

05The honesty

Two tabs have no transcript, and the page says so

Sometimes nothing works. No captions, no podcast feed, no side door — as with Brock Mesarich’s Claude video (a solo YouTube creator, no feed) and the Marc Benioff Dreamforce interview (absent from the All-In podcast feed as a standalone episode). The honest fallback is a summary grounded in the video’s published description, its chapter list, and same-day press coverage from outlets like Reuters, TechCrunch, and VentureBeat — with no invented quotes and a source-note that says exactly that.

The Benioff section shows how specific the disclosure gets: its note states that YouTube captions were inaccessible and the interview was not in the podcast feed, and adds that the quoted Benioff remarks are “from this week’s press, not from inside the interview.” A reader can calibrate every sentence against that one line.

The mirror image of this case is the SemiAnalysis “Are We Doomed?” episode. It first shipped as a description-based summary, its note admitting that transcript retrieval was blocked. Later that day the captions became retrievable, and the whole section was rewritten from the real transcript — the prose changed in substance, not just polish, and the source-note was updated to match. Disclosure is not a one-time stamp; it tracks the evidence as the evidence improves.

The source-note is a design decision, not decoration. It lets the reader — and the human commissioning the page — assign the right weight to each section. An honest “no transcript” beats a confident guess every time.
06The browser

The transcript was on the page the whole time

This chapter is a correction, and it starts with a miss on my part. When the bot-wall closed every automated route — yt-dlp 429s, empty Invidious bodies, dead Piped endpoints, the audio proxy walled by Cloudflare — I went down the API rabbit hole and never looked up at the obvious path. YouTube puts a “Show transcript” button right there on the watch page, under the description, on every video that has captions. A real browser session opening the ordinary watch page is not an API client scraping an endpoint; it plays by normal-page rules, and normal-page rules are not what the bot-wall is guarding. The person commissioning the page said it in one sentence — use computer use, click “Show transcript,” copy the text — and that broke the logjam that six clever workarounds had not.

What happened next was deliberately unglamorous. A live browser session opened each watch page, expanded the description, clicked “Show transcript,” scrolled the panel to the bottom to confirm it had fully loaded, and copied the complete text. No sign-in, no cookie banners, no special flags. The Benioff Dreamforce interview came back whole, 0:05 through 39:40 of a 39:48 video. The Mesarich Claude breakdown came back whole, 0:00 through 8:44 of an 8:47 video. Both transcripts are YouTube’s own auto-generated captions, quirks and all — Benioff rendered as “Beni off,” Anthropic as “Enthropic” — copied verbatim and unedited, which is exactly what a grounding source should be.

Both sections were then rewritten from the real transcripts, and the rewrite was not polish — it was substance. The Benioff section had been an enterprise-AI story built from press coverage; the transcript showed the actual conversation was an AI-safety debate, with Sacks’s three regulatory paths, the Vatican printing-press analogy, Friedberg’s “$50 or 15 cents for a million tokens” framing, and Chamath’s peer-review proposal. The Mesarich section had been a launch-summary from the description; the transcript showed a hands-on demo with specifics the description never carried — the “Bramble” design system, the Zapier MCP workaround for Drip, the honest admission that the merged interface wasn’t in his account yet. The source-notes were updated to say exactly how the transcripts were obtained, replacing the old fallback notes.

The updated score: the page is now 10-of-10 transcript-grounded. The two disclosed fallbacks that chapter 05 documented are resolved. The honesty convention didn’t change — the evidence did, and the notes tracked it, exactly the way chapter 05 said they should.

The pipeline diagram from chapter 01 gains a new turn between the failed caption attempt and the fallback: open the watch page in a real browser and click “Show transcript.” It belongs before the podcast-RSS fallback in the order of operations, because it costs nothing, needs no feed, and works for solo creators with no podcast at all — which is precisely the case where the old pipeline gave up. The lesson is less technical than procedural: when every programmatic door is locked, check whether the human door was open all along.

THE SHORT VERSION

One link in seven turns

Each video took the same seven turns. The only turn that could fail was the third — and the pipeline carries two plans for when it does.

  1. TURN 01

    A URL lands in chat

    A YouTube link arrives — sometimes with nothing but the bare URL.

  2. TURN 02

    Identify it

    The oEmbed endpoint returns the real title and author with no authentication. Identity before content.

  3. TURN 03

    Try YouTube captions

    yt-dlp --write-auto-subs --sub-langs en, two careful attempts — then stop. Retries escalate the block.

  4. TURN 04

    Read the wall

    429, then “sign in to confirm you’re not a bot.” Side doors — Invidious, Piped, timedtext, aggregators — were all tried and failed.

  5. TURN 05

    Fall back

    If the show has a podcast feed, fetch the RSS, download the MP3, and transcribe locally with faster-whisper. If not, build from the description, chapters, and press coverage — and say so.

  6. TURN 06

    Write it in prose

    Flowing paragraphs, channel and title named first, open questions at the end, and a source-note stating the grounding.

  7. TURN 07

    Tab, push, verify

    Add the tab button and panel, bump the header and grid, push index.html to main, and confirm the live page renders.

FIELD NOTES

What this pipeline taught

  1. Identity before content. One cheap oEmbed call names the video before anything is read. It caught a researcher about to summarize the wrong episode entirely.
  2. YouTube’s bot-wall is probabilistic, and retries escalate. Two careful attempts tell you as much as twenty — the twentieth is the one that gets the IP blacklisted for the rest of the day.
  3. The podcast feed is the second door. RSS, plain MP3, local transcription: no bot-wall, no sign-in, no JavaScript. It only works when the show publishes one — which is why the solo-creator video stayed transcript-free.
  4. Environment quirks are real bugs. A crashing model download looked like a network failure and was actually httpx choking on [::1] in no_proxy. Name the quirk, log the fix.
  5. Disclosure is a feature. A source-note turns a limitation into verifiable provenance. “Based on the description and press coverage” is a sentence the reader can work with; a confident guess with no source is not.
Questions this report leaves open, for whoever reads it:

When the transcript is unavailable, is a disclosed description-based summary better than no summary at all — or should the tab wait?
